What you actually have

Read the manifest before trusting any backtest built on it

The archive is a single-symbol, single-venue, top-of-book feed: every change to the best bid and offer on Nasdaq's own book, timestamped to the nanosecond, with sizes, order counts and the trade tape. That is genuinely high-grade data. It is also narrower than it first appears, in three ways that each kill a category of strategy:

  • One venue, not the market. Nasdaq clears ~$2.1 B of QQQ a day against roughly $15–20 B consolidated. You are seeing 10–15% of the book and you have no NBBO. Any spread measured here is wider than the spread a real order faces.
  • One symbol. No cross-section, so every classical statistical-arbitrage design — ranking, pairs, factor-neutral baskets — is off the table. You have a single time series.
  • No queue position. MBP-1 gives aggregate size at the touch, not individual orders. For a passive strategy your position in the queue is the strategy, and this schema cannot see it.

Everything below was measured on the real files — 65 days for bar-level tests, 20–24 days for event-level markouts, sampled evenly across the full two years so the results span two distinct volatility regimes.

The finding

Spread capture, and how fast it evaporates

The core question for any strategy that posts liquidity: you get filled at the touch, capturing half the spread — do you keep it? Measuring every passive fill against the mid at increasing horizons answers this directly.

You capture +0.159 bps at the moment of fill. It is gone in under one millisecond.

Consequence

An indiscriminate passive quoter in QQQ loses −0.074 bps per share at a one-second horizon. Nasdaq's top-tier add rebate ($0.00305/share ≈ +0.065 bps at $473) does not close the gap: net −0.015 bps.

Read that as a structural fact, not a bad day. Market making the most competitive ETF in the world is a rebate business with negative gross edge — and at the naive level, the rebate is not enough.

Why the obvious strategy fails

Strong signal, smaller than the toll

Queue imbalance predicts the next mid move about as well as anything in the microstructure literature — a Pearson IC of 0.236 at 100 ms is a very strong number by the standards of this field. It is also unmonetizable here, and the arithmetic is short.

The signal is real and the toll is bigger. This is what an efficient market looks like from the inside: prediction is almost good enough to pay for the spread, and never quite is. Any strategy whose first move is to cross the spread starts a third of a tick behind and does not catch up.

And the slower horizons are simply noise

Aggregating to one-minute bars across 65 days (25,350 bars) and testing every microstructure feature against 5-, 15-, 30- and 60-minute forward returns produces nothing that survives contact with a holdout.

There is no minutes-to-hours alpha in this dataset. Any model that appears to find one has fitted the 39 training days.

Where the edge actually is

The same fills, sorted by whether you should have wanted them

The markout number above averages over every fill. But a quoter chooses when to be in the book — and the average hides a clean, economically sensible split. Sorting 1.12 M passive fills by the state of the book immediately before each trade, one variable dominates: how wide the spread was when you quoted.

The sign flip

Quote everything and you lose. Quote only into a ≥ 2-tick spread with the queue leaning your way and the same mechanical strategy turns +0.048 bps — $0.226 per 100 shares.

The economics are intuitive once stated. At a one-tick spread you sit at the back of a deep queue behind faster participants; the only fills that reach you are the ones nobody faster wanted — you are being selected against, and that is exactly what the −0.099 bps says. At two ticks and wider you can quote inside, capture a genuinely larger spread, and compete against far fewer counterparties.

Note the last row: pushing imbalance past 0.8 breaks the edge. The relationship is non-monotonic, not a threshold — extreme imbalance means the book is about to flip, not that it is safe. That non-monotonicity is precisely the kind of structure a model learns and a hand-written rule does not, and it is the honest argument for using one.

What this is worth

The number to decide on, before writing any model code

The +0.048 bps above is a ceiling, not a forecast. It assumes you are filled on every qualifying trade, with zero latency, at a top-tier rebate that requires institutional volume to earn. Strip those and the picture is sobering.

Say it plainly

As a standalone business for one person, this does not clear its own infrastructure cost. Winning 50% of qualifying flow means beating Citadel and Virtu to the front of the queue, on their hardware budget. That is not a realistic entry point, and no model fixes it.

Which is why the plan below does not propose building a market-making firm. It proposes building the thing that is genuinely valuable, transferable and within reach — an execution and adverse-selection engine — and treating a live quoting strategy as an option you exercise only if the numbers earn it.

Two things here are worth real money without a single microsecond of colocation. First, the intraday cost curve is large and utterly stable: the Nasdaq spread runs 0.72 bps in the opening half-hour and 0.39–0.42 bps from 11:00 onward. Trading the open costs nearly double. Any order you send benefits from that immediately. Second, the adverse-selection model — given the book right now, will a passive fill here hurt me? — improves the execution of any strategy you already run, and it is the one component here that generalizes beyond QQQ.

The build

Five phases, ordered so the cheapest disproof comes first

The sequence is deliberate: each phase is capable of killing the project, and the ones most likely to kill it are earliest. Nothing here requires trading capital until phase 5.

01

Data spine

1–2 weeks

You have 45 GB of free disk and a 37.9 GB archive, so bulk extraction is impossible — the pipeline must stream. Read one day from the zip, decode, compute features, write a compact store, delete the raw file. This constraint is permanent; build for it now rather than discovering it at phase 3.

Deliver · Parallel day-level ETL (5 workers ≈ 3 s/day) → Parquet feature store, ~390 bars/day plus an event-level fill table. Full 515-day run in under an hour.
Watch · df['flags'] not df.flags (it collides with pandas' own DataFrame.flags); RTH is 390 minutes and shifts UTC offset across DST; filter to F_LAST rows for coherent book states.
02

Replicate at full scale

1 week

Re-run every number in this memo across all 515 days, split by year and by volatility regime. The markout sign flip is the entire thesis; if it is an artifact of the 20 sampled days, the project ends here and you have spent three weeks instead of three months.

Deliver · Regime-split markout tables; a per-day time series of the conditional edge; a stability chart of the +0.048 bps figure over two years.
Kill if · The wide-spread edge is not positive in both years independently.
03

The model — and its target

2–4 weeks

This is the phase where most attempts fail, and the failure is always the same: they train a model to predict the direction of the mid. Section two shows why that is a dead end — direction is predictable at IC 0.24 and still loses money, so a better directional model just loses more efficiently.

Predict the P&L of an action, not the path of a price. The label is the realized markout in bps of a passive fill posted in the current book state. It is economically denominated, directly decision-relevant, and you have roughly 25 million labelled fills to learn it from.

Features · spread state, microprice offset, queue imbalance and its non-monotone transforms, order-flow imbalance over 100 ms / 1 s / 10 s, trade intensity, time since last trade, top-of-book order counts, realized volatility, time of day.
Models · Gradient boosting as the baseline that must be beaten; a small temporal CNN over the raw event stream as the challenger.
Validation · Purged walk-forward by day with a one-day embargo. Score in bps of P&L, never accuracy or AUC.
Kill if · The model cannot beat the two-line rule from the previous section out of sample. If a rule you can write on a napkin wins, ship the napkin.
04

Honest fill simulation

2–3 weeks

Every number in this memo assumes you were filled on each qualifying trade. You would not have been. MBP-1 cannot see your queue position, so the simulator must model it: enter at the back of the displayed size, advance on cancels ahead of you, and require traded volume at your level to exceed your inferred position before you fill.

Expect this phase to cut the edge substantially — that is the point of running it. Use the real fee schedule for the tier you would actually qualify for, not the top tier.

Deliver · Queue-position simulator with latency injection (0.5 ms, 5 ms, 50 ms); P&L as a function of latency and rebate tier; a fill-rate sensitivity curve.
Kill if · The edge does not survive a realistic fill rate at a realistic rebate tier — the most likely outcome, and worth knowing in three months rather than three years.
05

Decide

1 week

Three honest outcomes, and only one of them is trade it.

A · Ship the execution layer. The likeliest and still genuinely valuable result: deploy the adverse-selection model as passive/aggressive order routing for whatever you already trade. Real bps, no colocation, no new risk.
B · Buy the missing data. If the edge survives phase 4, the binding constraint is data, not modelling — see below. Re-run before committing capital.
C · Stop. Write up the negative result. A measured, documented “this market is efficient at my latency” is a real research output, and it protects you from re-running this in a year.

What you would have to buy

The three gaps, in order of how much they matter

If phase 4 clears, do not scale on this dataset — its limits become binding well before capital does.

  • MBO (full order book). The critical one. Individual order add/cancel/execute messages give true queue position, which is the difference between a simulated fill and a real one. Without it, phase 4 is an estimate.
  • Consolidated / NBBO tape. Turns a Nasdaq-local view into a market view, and lets you tell a genuinely wide market from a Nasdaq-only quote gap — which is what some of the “spread ≥ 2 ticks” opportunity may turn out to be.
  • More symbols. The wide-spread edge should be larger in less-contested names. QQQ is the hardest venue you could have picked; the same model on mid-cap ETFs is where this thesis is genuinely worth testing.

Standing kill criteria

Write these down before you start, so the decision is made while you are still objective:

  • The wide-spread edge fails to hold in both years independently → stop.
  • The learned model cannot beat the two-line rule out of sample → ship the rule, drop the model.
  • Realistic queue simulation at a realistic rebate tier turns the edge negative → stop, and keep the execution layer.
  • Any result that needs the top rebate tier to be positive → treat as negative. You will not have that tier.
